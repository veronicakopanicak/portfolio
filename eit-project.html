01 / THE CONCEPT

Project overview

My contributions: Built the eight-electrode cylindrical wearable EIT model (Model 2) from scratch in COMSOL, developed its multilayer tissue geometry, configured the electrodes, ran tumour-position parametric simulations, and analyzed the resulting voltage deviations. Model 1 was developed by my teammate and is included for comparison.

Electrical Impedance Tomography (EIT) is a non-invasive imaging technique that uses surface electrical measurements to infer variations in the conductivity and permittivity of tissue beneath the surface.

This project explored whether a simulated abnormality could be detected by modelling how electrical signals travel through healthy tissue and tissue containing a tumour. Two sensor configurations were investigated:

  • A four-electrode handheld probe for localized measurements.
  • An eight-electrode cylindrical wearable sensor for measurements across multilayer tissue.

Comparing the sensor configurations

MODEL 1 · TEAMMATE'S WORK

Handheld probe

Four electrodes for localized, directional surface measurements. Intended for targeted scanning where the probe can be repositioned.

MODEL 2 · MY WORK

Cylindrical wearable

Eight circumferential electrodes for measurements around a multilayer tissue cross-section. Designed as a simulated wearable concept for limb-like geometries.

Both designs were evaluated computationally; neither was fabricated or clinically validated.

02 / MODEL 1 / HANDHELD PROBE

Model 1: Handheld EIT probe

Developed by my teammate, included here for context.

Design

The first model places four circular surface electrodes on a block of tissue. A 1 V AC signal is applied between two diagonal electrodes, and the other two electrodes passively measure the resulting voltage. The model was adapted from a COMSOL base model (Electric Sensor #211).

  • Tissue block: 0.2 m × 0.2 m × 0.2 m
  • Electrodes: four circular surface patches, 2 cm radius
  • Baseline frequency: 100 kHz
  • Healthy tissue: conductivity 0.2 S/m, relative permittivity 10,000
  • Tumour inclusion: conductivity 0.6 S/m, relative permittivity 20,000

Simulation and analysis

My teammate ran frequency-domain simulations with COMSOL’s Electric Currents interface, with and without the tumour. Detection was measured as the percentage change in the differential voltage between the two sensing electrodes. They then varied tumour depth, tumour size, and electrode placement to see how each affected the signal.

Results

The simulations showed a measurable change in the differential electrode voltage when the tumour was introduced, and that change depended on tumour depth, tumour size, and electrode placement.

03 / MODEL 2 / MY WORK

Model 2: Wearable EIT sensor — my work

Design

Why this design? The cylindrical configuration distributed electrodes around the tissue rather than concentrating them on one surface. I used this geometry to investigate how measured voltages varied as the simulated tumour moved through different positions in the multilayer tissue model.

I built the eight-electrode cylindrical sensor model from scratch in COMSOL Multiphysics. I constructed a multilayer cylindrical tissue domain, arranged the electrodes around its perimeter, and included a spherical tumour region to investigate how its position influenced the electrical response.

  • Cylinder: 10 cm radius, 5 cm height
  • Eight electrodes spaced 45° apart: E1 injects current, E5 is ground, and E2–E4 and E6–E8 are voltage-measurement electrodes
  • Layers: outer fat, a muscle ring, and a central bone, with muscle and bone properties from COMSOL’s material library
  • Spherical tumour inclusion, 2 cm radius (conductivity 0.4 S/m, relative permittivity 60)
  • Applied current: 1 mA AC at 100 kHz

Simulation and analysis

A parametric sweep moved the tumour across eight positions from x = −0.07 m to x = +0.07 m. I compared each electrode’s voltage with the healthy-tissue baseline to calculate the relative signal deviation caused by the tumour.

Results

MODEL 2 · PEAK SIMULATED DEVIATION
22.2%

Peak relative voltage deviation at electrode E4 when the simulated tumour was at x = +0.07 m, compared with the healthy-tissue baseline. This is a model-specific simulation result, not a measure of clinical detection accuracy.

The tumour’s position changed the simulated voltage response across the electrode array. At x = +0.07 m, the largest reported deviation was approximately 22.2% at electrode E4, while other electrodes also showed substantial changes relative to the healthy-tissue baseline. This supports position-dependent sensitivity within the simulated geometry, not verified clinical detection accuracy.

Effect of tissue complexity

The final presentation also compared a simplified tissue-only configuration with the multilayer model containing muscle and bone. The reported peak deviations were approximately 66.6% for the tissue-only configuration and 23.9% for the multilayer configuration. The lower, more distributed response in the multilayer simulation illustrates why tissue composition matters when interpreting electrode measurements. These are simulated values for different model configurations, not a direct measure of clinical performance.

04 / DESIGN DECISIONS

Engineering challenges and design decisions

Building the wearable model from scratch

The main challenge was turning a wearable-sensor concept into a workable electrical simulation. I chose a cylindrical domain with circumferential electrodes and a movable spherical inclusion so I could examine how sensor geometry affected the measurements.

Accounting for multilayer tissue geometry

A single uniform tissue region would miss the influence of different current paths. I therefore included fat, muscle, and bone regions and compared the multilayer response with a simpler tissue-only model to understand how additional anatomical detail changed the signals.

Evaluating sensitivity to tumour position

Because one voltage reading could not describe the whole sensor response, I compared all measurement electrodes against a healthy-tissue baseline at eight tumour positions. This made it possible to identify position-dependent trends and assess the limits of the simulated results.

05 / PROJECT SCOPE

Limitations

This project was computational only. It involved no clinical testing and no fabricated sensor. The models use simplified tissue geometries and material properties, the tumour shape in Model 1 is inherited from the base model, and measurement noise and real electrode contact conditions were not evaluated. The results show simulated sensitivity to tissue-property changes, not validated clinical tumour detection.

06 / REFLECTION

Key takeaways

This project taught me how to move from a sensor concept to a testable simulation—and how much the modelling assumptions affect the interpretation of results.

  • Modelling decisions: Geometry, electrode placement, and tissue properties all shape the predicted electrical response.
  • Simulation workflow: Parametric sweeps and baseline comparisons helped turn raw electrode voltages into interpretable trends.
  • Critical evaluation: A promising simulated signal is not the same as a clinically validated sensor; real-world testing remains essential.
