Electrical Impedance Tomography Sensor Simulation

Engineering Physics design project · McMaster University · 2025

A team project simulating two electrical impedance tomography sensor configurations in COMSOL Multiphysics to investigate non-invasive tumour detection through electrical property variations in biological tissue.

  • COMSOL Multiphysics
  • EIT
  • Finite Element Modelling
  • Parametric Simulation
  • Data Analysis

Project overview

Electrical Impedance Tomography (EIT) is a non-invasive imaging technique that uses surface electrical measurements to infer variations in the conductivity and permittivity of tissue beneath the surface.

This project explored whether a simulated abnormality could be detected by modelling how electrical signals travel through healthy tissue and tissue containing a tumour. Two sensor configurations were investigated:

  • A four-electrode handheld probe for localized measurements.
  • An eight-electrode cylindrical wearable sensor for measurements across multilayer tissue.

The work involved building computational models, assigning electrical material properties, running parametric simulations, and analyzing voltage responses to compare sensitivity.

Model 1: Handheld EIT probe

Developed by my teammate [teammate’s name], included here for context.

Design

The first model places four circular surface electrodes on a block of tissue. A 1 V AC signal is applied between two diagonal electrodes, and the other two electrodes passively measure the resulting voltage. The model was adapted from a COMSOL base model (Electric Sensor #211).

  • Tissue block: 0.2 m × 0.2 m × 0.2 m
  • Electrodes: four circular surface patches, 2 cm radius
  • Baseline frequency: 100 kHz
  • Healthy tissue: conductivity 0.2 S/m, relative permittivity 10,000
  • Tumour inclusion: conductivity 0.6 S/m, relative permittivity 20,000

Simulation and analysis

My teammate ran frequency-domain simulations with COMSOL’s Electric Currents interface, with and without the tumour. Detection was measured as the percentage change in the differential voltage between the two sensing electrodes. They then varied tumour depth, tumour size, and electrode placement to see how each affected the signal.

Model 2: Wearable EIT sensor (my work)

Design

The second model arranges eight electrodes around a cylindrical, multilayer tissue domain to see how a more complex geometry changes the electrical measurements.

  • Cylinder: 10 cm radius, 5 cm height
  • Eight electrodes spaced 45° apart; one injects current, one is ground, the rest measure voltage
  • Layers: outer fat, a muscle ring, and a central bone, with muscle and bone properties from COMSOL’s material library
  • Spherical tumour inclusion, 2 cm radius (conductivity 0.4 S/m, relative permittivity 60)
  • Applied current: 1 mA AC at 100 kHz

Simulation and analysis

A parametric sweep moved the tumour across eight positions from x = −0.07 m to x = +0.07 m. I compared each electrode’s voltage with the healthy-tissue baseline to calculate the relative signal deviation caused by the tumour.

Results

Tumour position clearly changed the voltage response across the electrode array. At the closest position (x = +0.07 m), the deviation at the measuring electrodes ranged from about 10% to 22%, and the largest values were around 22%. Electrode placement, tissue geometry, and the location of the conductivity change all shaped the measured signal.

Engineering challenges and design decisions

Sensor configuration

We compared two electrode arrangements to see how sensor geometry affects measurement. The handheld probe targets localized measurements, while the cylindrical array covers a larger tissue cross-section.

Tissue modelling

We assigned different conductivity and permittivity values to each tissue region so the simulations could show how fields and voltages change when an abnormal region appears.

Parametric analysis

We used parametric sweeps to study how tumour depth (Model 1) and position (Model 2) influenced the response, and compared results to identify trends in sensitivity.

Limitations

This project was computational only. It involved no clinical testing and no fabricated sensor. The models use simplified tissue geometries and material properties, the tumour shape in Model 1 is inherited from the base model, and measurement noise and real electrode contact conditions were not evaluated. The results show simulated sensitivity to tissue-property changes, not validated clinical tumour detection.
